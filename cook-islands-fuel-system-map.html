SYSTEM VULNERABILITIES & LEVERAGE POINTS

Key findings from the Cook Islands fuel supply map, in plain language.
VULNERABILITY 01

Everything begins in Singapore

Virtually all petroleum products consumed in the Cook Islands originate from Singapore refineries. Any disruption to Singapore refining capacity, shipping routes, or pricing has immediate and total downstream effects. There is no meaningful alternative upstream source at present.

VULNERABILITY 02

Fiji is the only real regional hub

Fiji bulk terminals act as the primary redistribution node for Cook Islands supply. New Zealand provides an alternative corridor, but at smaller volumes. If Fiji terminals face disruption — cyclone, labour dispute, equipment failure — Cook Islands supply is severely compromised within weeks.

VULNERABILITY 03

Rarotonga port and storage is a single chokepoint

Avatiu Harbour is the sole commercial port of entry. The bulk storage cluster immediately behind it holds only 30–60 days of supply under normal demand. A cyclone, a missed tanker shipment, or a storage failure creates an island-wide shortage with no backup.

VULNERABILITY 04

Outer islands live at the end of a very long, fragile chain

The northern group atolls receive fuel only in drums, delivered by inter-island vessels that may call monthly or less. Any disruption to Taio Shipping or similar operators leaves communities with no supply at all.

VULNERABILITY 05

Global price volatility passes straight through

With no domestic refining, no strategic buffer stock, and long supply chains, Cook Islands consumers absorb global oil price movements in full — amplified by shipping costs. The government carries significant fiscal exposure through fuel subsidies and TAU diesel generation costs.

LEVERAGE POINTS ⚡

Three places to intervene for the greatest systemic effect.
LEVERAGE POINT 01

Diversify the supply corridor

Develop direct Singapore–Rarotonga shipping or expand the NZ corridor. Each additional supply route reduces dependency on any single node and improves negotiating leverage on price.

LEVERAGE POINT 02

Increase strategic storage — especially outer islands

Expanding bulk storage at Rarotonga from ~30 to 90+ days transforms resilience. On outer islands, even modest solar-plus-battery microgrids reduce diesel demand enough to cut resupply frequency from monthly to quarterly.

LEVERAGE POINT 03

Reduce demand at the power station and in transport

TAU diesel generation is the largest single consumer in the system. Displacing it with solar, wind, and battery storage cuts imports, electricity cost, and carbon exposure. EV adoption and commercial fuel-switching amplifies the effect across the transport segment.
