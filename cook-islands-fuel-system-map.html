SYSTEM VULNERABILITIES & LEVERAGE POINTS

Key findings from the Cook Islands fuel supply map, in plain language.
CRITICAL VULNERABILITY 01

Singapore serves larger markets first — Cook Islands is last in the queue

Singapore refineries have finite output and finite tanker availability. When global demand is high or refinery capacity is constrained, they prioritise high-volume buyers: Japan, Korea, China, India, Australia. Pacific island nations are residual-allocation customers. The Cook Islands does not have long-term supply contracts that guarantee volume — it takes what is left. This means disruptions elsewhere in the world can cut Cook Islands supply before a single local factor changes.

CRITICAL VULNERABILITY 02

The Tahiti route is not diversification — it is the same dependency

French Polynesia (Tahiti) sources its refined petroleum from Singapore via medium-range tankers — the exact same upstream supply chain as Fiji and New Zealand. The Tahiti corridor to the northern atolls offers a different shipping path but zero upstream independence. If Singapore supply tightens, Tahiti faces the same shortage at the same time.

VULNERABILITY 03

Rarotonga port and storage is a single chokepoint

Avatiu Harbour is the only commercial port of entry. The bulk storage cluster immediately behind it holds only 30–60 days of supply. A cyclone, a missed tanker, or a storage failure creates an island-wide shortage with no backup port and no strategic reserve.

CRITICAL VULNERABILITY 04

When jet fuel runs short, flights cancel — and the whole economy follows

A fuel shortage does not just mean generators slow and cars queue at service stations. When Jet A-1 stocks fall below airline minimums, carriers cancel flights. That means tourists cannot arrive — the single largest revenue source for the Cook Islands vanishes. It also means cargo flights and air freight routes are cut, blocking time-sensitive imports (medicine, fresh food, parts) and exports. The aviation cascade turns a fuel logistics problem into a full economic crisis within days.

VULNERABILITY 05

Outer islands live at the end of a very long, fragile chain

The northern group atolls receive fuel only in drums, via vessels that may call monthly or less. Any disruption to Taio Shipping or similar operators leaves communities with no supply at all. And because Tahiti is Singapore-dependent, that route offers no real backup.

VULNERABILITY 06

Global price volatility passes straight through

With no domestic refining, no strategic buffer stock, and long supply chains, Cook Islands consumers absorb global oil price movements in full — amplified by freight costs. The government carries significant fiscal exposure through fuel subsidies and TAU diesel generation.

LEVERAGE POINTS ⚡

Three places to intervene for the greatest systemic effect.
LEVERAGE POINT 01

Secure long-term supply contracts and diversify corridors

Negotiate volume-guaranteed contracts with Singapore refineries or alternative refiners (Middle East, Australia). Develop direct Singapore–Rarotonga shipping and expand the NZ corridor. Priority customer status provides a buffer when global allocation tightens.

LEVERAGE POINT 02

Increase strategic storage — especially jet fuel

Expanding bulk storage at Rarotonga from ~30 to 90+ days — with ring-fenced strategic Jet A-1 reserves — prevents the aviation cascade from triggering. Outer island solar-battery microgrids reduce inter-island drum runs from monthly to quarterly, compounding resilience gains.

LEVERAGE POINT 03

Reduce demand at the power station and across transport

TAU diesel generation is the largest single consumer. Displacing it with solar, wind, and battery storage cuts imports, cost, and carbon in one move. EV adoption and commercial fuel-switching compounds the effect — and shrinks the aviation cascade trigger threshold by reducing baseline diesel demand.
